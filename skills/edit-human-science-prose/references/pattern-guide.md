# Contextual anti-slop pattern guide

Use these as signals to inspect, not automatic deletion rules.

## Usually repair

### Empty setup

Examples include throat clearing, faux insider claims, or stage directions that delay the point: "Here is the thing," "what nobody tells you," or "let that sink in."

Repair by stating the thought unless the setup reveals character, uncertainty, or genuine narrative tension.

### Inflated significance

Watch for claims that something is pivotal, profound, transformative, essential, or groundbreaking without showing why.

Repair by naming the concrete consequence. Let the reader judge its importance.

### Vague authority

Watch for "experts agree," "research shows," or "many believe."

Repair with the supplied source, evidence type, or bounded attribution. Flag the claim if support is absent.

### Abstract action

Watch for weak constructions such as "serves as," "functions as," "plays a role in," "facilitates," and abstract nouns doing human work.

Prefer a precise verb or a plain `is` when accurate.

### Superficial interpretation

Watch for trailing clauses using words such as "highlighting," "underscoring," "showcasing," or "reflecting" to attach generic meaning to a fact.

Replace them with a real mechanism or consequence if the supplied material supports one. Otherwise stop at the fact.

### Synonym cycling

Do not rename the same concept repeatedly to avoid repetition. Repeat the correct term unless the meaning truly changes.

### Robotic symmetry

Watch for repeated paragraph templates, serial three-part lists, stacked fragments, identical sentence lengths, and recurring contrast formulas.

Vary structure according to the reasoning. Do not introduce random variation as camouflage.

### Manufactured revelation

Watch for colon reveals, self-answered rhetorical questions, "plot twist" language, premature declarations of surprise, and inflated endings.

Earn revelation through evidence and sequence. State the insight plainly once earned.

### Decorative polish

Watch for needless headings, excessive bullets, bold fragments, slogans, tidy recap endings, and quotable final lines that oversimplify the chapter.

Use structure only when it helps the reader navigate real complexity.

## Judge in context

The following can be excellent long-form tools:

- binary contrast;
- negative construction;
- rhetorical question;
- sentence fragment;
- em dash;
- repeated phrase;
- lyrical ending;
- first-person aside;
- delayed thesis.

Flag them only when they become habitual, manipulative, inaccurate, or interchangeable with generic AI prose.

## Word choice

Do not maintain a universal banned-word list. Inspect fashionable abstractions and inflated verbs such as `delve`, `leverage`, `robust`, `transformative`, `multifaceted`, `elevate`, `harness`, and `streamline`. Replace them when they obscure a more precise word. Keep any word that is accurate, necessary, or characteristic of the author.

Cut filler adverbs and phrases when they add nothing. Preserve them when they express real emphasis, contrast, uncertainty, or spoken cadence.

## Detect-mode report

For each meaningful issue, use:

|---|---|---|---|

Group repeated instances of the same habit. Prioritize patterns that affect truth, clarity, voice, or reading momentum.
