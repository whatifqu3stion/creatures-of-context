# Chapter 14
## The world we hand on

A day-care center can close at four. That does not make every parent arrive by four.

At ten centers in Haifa, Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini recorded late pickups for four weeks. Six randomly selected centers then introduced a fine of ten shekels for arriving more than ten minutes late. Four continued without it.

Late pickups increased where the fine was imposed. Removing it did not undo the change during the four remaining weeks of observation.[^14e]

What had the parents been charged for? The authors considered whether the fee had made lateness look like an extra service with a price. That remained an interpretation of the result. The experiment could not tell them what every parent was thinking, or what a larger fine would have done.

A rule enters a world in which people are already interpreting one another. It can change what an action costs and what the action seems to mean. The people who inherit the rule may encounter both changes without knowing how either began.

### What we do not have to discover again

An ordinary recipe contains knowledge its reader did not have to discover.

Somebody learned what could be eaten, how ingredients behaved, which sequence worked, and how to communicate enough of the result for another person to begin. The recipe may have changed many times. The cook can change it again.

Reading a timetable requires shared symbols and conventions. Using a medicine depends on knowledge, institutions, and the labor of people one will never meet. Even disagreeing with an inherited idea requires language and methods acquired from others. Individual ability has a world of accumulated work to act upon.

Alex Mesoudi and Alex Thornton distinguish cumulative cultural evolution from transmission alone. In their account, changes are socially passed on, and further modifications can produce successive improvements in a particular performance. They also emphasize that researchers use the term in different ways and should specify what they are claiming to demonstrate.[^14a]

The qualification matters. A practice can spread because it is familiar, enforced, or useful to influential people. Even improved performance leaves a question: better at what? A method can become more effective at surveillance without improving the lives of those watched.

A cook can often taste the result. Many cultural consequences are harder to inspect. Benefits can arrive for one group while costs are borne elsewhere. A familiar arrangement may keep working well for the people with the least reason to question it.

### The lesson underneath the lesson

An organization can say that asking for help is encouraged while rewarding only people who appear never to need it. A family can say that disagreement is welcome while withdrawing affection when it occurs. A school can praise curiosity and leave no time for a question outside the schedule.

These imagined cases contain two lessons: the stated rule and the pattern of consequences. A newcomer has to discover which one predicts what will happen.

Consider a workplace that says it values rest. That promise eventually meets a deadline. Somebody must decide whether to reduce the work, pay for more help, delay delivery, or ask the existing staff to stay. Employees have something more substantial to interpret after that decision than they had after the announcement.

People do not simply mirror a setting. They resist, improvise, and bring different histories into the same room. But if a workplace repeatedly punishes the conduct it officially welcomes, an employee’s reluctance should not surprise us. The institution has been teaching too.

### A value needs somewhere to appear

Some shared practices mark transitions, hold attention, and make participation visible. They give people a way to do something together when an instruction would feel inadequate.

Nicholas Hobson and colleagues’ review organizes research on ritual around possible functions in emotion, performance goals, and social connection. Their framework considers features of a practice alongside the meanings participants give it.[^14b] It offers questions to investigate, not a guarantee that repeating a sequence will produce a desired feeling.

Consider a meal at which an absent family member is remembered. The food may be ordinary. The deliberate place given to remembrance changes what the gathering asks of those present. A newcomer’s welcome can work similarly: explaining a group’s history and inviting a contribution give membership a visible beginning.

Such practices offer continuity without requiring everyone to invent the moment alone. Participation can also be difficult. Someone may be unable to take part, disagree with the meaning, or feel obliged to display a feeling they do not have.

The practical questions are close at hand. Can the practice accommodate different capacities? Is refusal possible? Whose history is remembered, and who does the preparation? A celebration of care is thin if the people doing the caring are too exhausted to attend.

### After the meeting

The garden from the previous chapter now has a month of experience with its revised division of work. In this imagined continuation, assigning different tasks has helped. The caregiver can arrange swaps from home, and the member who struggled with digging has taken shorter watering visits. Fewer jobs go unfilled.

One cost becomes apparent only when somebody asks about the record. Maintaining it, answering messages, and finding replacements have taken much longer than expected. The group has made room for different contributions but still failed to count one of them properly.

If it simply congratulates itself on becoming fairer, the hidden work will remain hidden. Instead, the members set a limit on the coordination role and take turns doing it. At the end of the trial, they also reduce the number of beds they cultivate. There will be less produce. There is no longer quite so much unpaid rescue work required to keep the promise.

Respecting the members’ limits has meant growing less. The rule has changed what the garden can promise, including to people who hoped for more from it.

Actual communities have faced much larger versions of this problem. Elinor Ostrom studied how they managed shared resources. She examined how people devised and maintained different arrangements, including governance by resource users. Participation in making rules, monitoring, and workable ways to resolve conflict were among the features her work brought into view.[^14c] The work challenged the idea that shared resources must always be managed through one universal form.

It did not discover naturally harmonious communities. Rules need to fit the resource and the people, and that fit requires attention over time. Local knowledge is valuable; local power can still be unfair. A small scale does not make an arrangement just.

The gardeners’ first equal-hours rule made obligations clear. Their second arrangement made different capacities easier to accommodate. Neither ended the work of judgment. Who benefits from the reduced harvest? Who can attend the review meeting? Who is believed when they say a task takes longer than the record shows?

Those questions belong inside the institution, where people can answer them and change a decision. If they exist only in private complaints, the public rule may look successful while the costs accumulate elsewhere.

### Who gets to speak for a culture?

Culture is often described as though everyone in a country or community shares one mind. The phrase *our culture* can conceal an argument about whose practices count and who gets to name the tradition.

Research can repeat that mistake. Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine, and Ara Norenzayan drew attention to the heavy reliance of behavioral science on Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations.[^14d] Evidence from a narrow sample should not quietly become a claim about all people. Nor does finding a difference between groups make either group internally uniform.

Within a family, workplace, religious community, or nation, people inherit different positions. They can disagree about a practice while appealing to a shared value. A younger member may ask to change a tradition precisely because they believe its promise should include someone previously left out.

Cultural participation therefore involves more than a choice between accepting an inheritance and discarding it. What is worth keeping? Whose experience has been omitted? Which form no longer serves the value it claims to express? Sometimes the answer is preservation. Sometimes refusal. The history cannot make that decision for us, but understanding it can clarify what is at stake.

### What the next person meets

A newcomer to the garden will find fewer beds, a rota, and people who remember why the arrangements changed. If the explanation survives, the newcomer can learn from the disagreement as well as from the rule. If only the rule survives, it may eventually look arbitrary.

The same question faces larger institutions: how will they preserve useful learning without making an old solution impossible to challenge?

Whoever enters a room tomorrow will encounter choices made before they arrive. Someone has set the schedule, placed the furniture, chosen which mistakes to record, and established how disagreement is received. Some decisions were deliberate. Others accumulated without anyone considering them together.

Our power over those conditions differs enormously. A manager, planner, parent, participant, and newcomer do not possess the same authority or resources. Responsibility should follow those differences. Wherever influence exists, though, there is a question to ask: what does this arrangement make people carry, and what does it make possible?

A change may be small: explaining an ambiguous message, sharing a recurring task, giving someone a credible way to correct a mistake. It may require funding, collective action, or a different rule. Its scale should follow the problem.

The garden has not solved cooperation. It has learned something about its own demands and left a way for the next group of members to learn more. That is one useful thing a culture can pass on: a practice, a memory of what it cost, and permission to reconsider it.
