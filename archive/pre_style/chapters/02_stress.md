# Chapter 2
## What stress hides

One of the more reliable ways to elicit a cortisol response from a healthy adult
in a laboratory is to ask them to give a speech.

The volunteer stands in front of a panel and speaks while the panel watches
without encouragement. Then comes mental arithmetic, performed aloud while
errors are corrected and the task begins again.

Researchers call this the Trier Social Stress Test. Its ingredients are
ordinary enough: difficult work, public judgment, and very little control over
the outcome. Sally Dickerson and Margaret Kemeny brought together laboratory studies to
examine which demands produce this hormonal response. Social evaluation and
limited control were a particularly potent combination, though these features
also overlapped with effort and unpredictability.[^2-1]

There is no physical attack. Nothing has gone wrong yet. But something is at
stake. The volunteer is trying to perform while other people decide what that
performance says about them.

The usual story about stress says that the body is ancient, modern life is
confusing, and an email or audience can set off the same alarm as a predator.
It is an appealing story because it makes a strong reaction feel less absurd.
It also makes the body seem rather stupid.

People do not need to mistake a panel for teeth. Judgment can affect work,
belonging, reputation, money, and the private confidence that one can do what
is being asked. The threat is not the same as an attack. The consequences can
still matter.

The word *stress* can cover useful preparation, prolonged strain, and
responses that no longer fit a situation. By itself, it does not tell us
which of these is happening, or what would help.

### One word, several events

During the Trier test, researchers can collect saliva and watch cortisol rise
and fall. Cortisol participates in the body's ordinary work of adjusting
energy and other processes. It also changes across the day, with waking,
activity, illness, medication, and much else besides. A saliva sample tells us
how one chemical response changed during one chosen stretch of time. It does
not contain the experience in miniature.

The rest of the person has not stood still while the sample was collected.
Heart activity may change. Attention may tighten around mistakes and signs of
judgment. The volunteer may feel anxious, irritated, embarrassed, sharply
focused, or determined to do well. They may want to escape, argue, try harder,
or go blank.

These changes are related. They are not one event repeated in different
languages. In a review of Trier-test studies, cortisol and unpleasant emotion
both changed on average, but the size of one change did not reliably tell
researchers the size of the other.[^2-2] Someone can feel terrible without
producing the largest cortisol rise. Someone with a large rise need not report
the most distress.

Neither person is getting stress wrong. Feeling describes one part of what is
happening. A hormone describes another. Attention, action, and what the person
does the next time belong to the response too.

Outside the laboratory, life is less considerate about holding its variables
still. In studies that repeatedly sampled people during ordinary days,
reported stress and changes in cortisol, heart rate, or blood pressure often
failed to line up neatly. Walking upstairs, drinking coffee, sleeping badly,
taking medicine, arguing with a partner, and worrying about tomorrow all
arrive in the same body.[^2-3]

The dream of a single reading is understandable. If someone has been told that
their suffering is exaggerated, an objective number can look like a witness
who will finally be believed. Yet the same number can be turned against them.
The reading is low, so nothing serious is happening. The reading is high, so
the person must be damaged. Neither conclusion follows.

The word *stress* invites this confusion because it sounds like the name of
one thing. We use it for a fever, an argument, a hard workout, debt, grief,
noise, public speaking, and the wait for a medical result. Those experiences
can share parts of a response. They do not create the same problem.

Barbra Streisand has described how the fear of forgetting lyrics shaped her
life as a performer. In 2005, she traced it to a lapse during a 1967 concert in
Central Park and said that fear of repeating the experience helped keep her
from charging for live performances for twenty-seven years. Later, she linked the fear to a co-star's behavior during *Funny Girl*.[^2-4]

The accounts were given years apart and do not produce a tidy first cause. They tell us how Streisand later described the fear and its history. They do not establish which mechanism caused it, or how much each experience contributed.

This book calls that work of noticing and preparing for possible harm
*Threat*. Threat is a lens, not a brain switch. It draws our attention to the
relationship between the person and what might happen: what experience has
taught them to expect, what they are trying to protect, and what options they
have now.

An alarm response is not a verdict. It is preparation.

### Preparation can help—and still cost us

Preparation does not have to feel good to be useful. A driver who sees a car
move unexpectedly may need a faster heartbeat and a fierce narrowing of
attention. The same narrowing can make a familiar name disappear just when a
speaker needs it. A response can help with one part of a situation and make
another part harder.

Its size cannot settle whether it is healthy. A large response is not
automatically evidence of damage. A small one is not automatically resilience.
What does it help the person do, and what does it begin to crowd out?

Sometimes the cost is immediate. Panic makes it hard to speak. Anger intended to defend
against humiliation may ruin a conversation. Vigilance catches a real danger
and makes sleep impossible afterward.

Sometimes the demand keeps returning. A caregiver remains responsible through
the night. A worker has more tasks than time and no authority to change either.
A tenant does not know whether the next notice will threaten their home. A
family waits for a result that will decide what happens next. In these lives,
alarm is not simply stuck after the danger has passed. The situation keeps
asking for attention.

Over time, the cost can appear in several places. Sleep frays. Worry takes up
room needed for other thought. Pain changes movement. People may drink more,
eat differently, withdraw, or lose the activities that once helped them feel
restored. Changes in hormones, the heart and blood vessels, immunity, and the
nervous system may join that picture. They do not all move together, and the
long-term story is not one hormone left running.[^2-5]

Even *recovery* becomes slippery if we demand one biological score. After a
laboratory challenge, researchers can calculate cortisol's return in several
reasonable ways, and those calculations can disagree about the same response.
Outside the lab, the thing we hope to get back is easier to recognize. Can the
person sleep? Can they think about something else? Do appetite and ordinary
energy return? Can their attention become available for someone they love?
Most of all, has the situation stopped requiring vigilance?[^2-6]

That last question is not under private control. Shift work, pain, insecure
housing, unpaid care, discrimination, illness, and continuing danger can leave
nowhere for the demand to end. A person can use every calming technique they
know and still wake inside the same problem.

Settling enough to sleep, think, or avoid hurting someone can still matter
enormously. At times it is the only reachable change. It just isn't always the
same job as solving the problem.

### What would reach the problem?

Imagine waiting for a medical result. Being told the exact hour of the phone
call can end one uncertainty. It cannot change the result. Imagine receiving
excellent feedback on work that still cannot fit into the available hours. The
work has become clearer, not more possible. A friend saying “You've got this”
may be kind. A friend picking up the children may change what the evening
allows.

We often call all of these things *control*, but they are different. Knowing
what will happen is predictability. Learning whether an action worked is
feedback. Believing you can handle the situation is confidence. Actual control
means that something you do changes what happens.

In one experiment, participants faced unpleasant noise and electrical
stimulation. Some had an action that could stop it; others did not. The working
option reduced helplessness, though it did not erase every unpleasant feeling.
A separate exercise using positive feedback and memories of past competence
failed to increase measured self-efficacy or improve affect. It therefore could
not settle whether a successful increase in confidence would have helped.[^2-7]

The distinction survives that uncertainty. Feeling able to act and having an
action that changes the outcome are questions worth asking separately.

Other people can sometimes provide the option one person lacks. They can share
a night shift, make a call, lend money, give a ride, offer witness, use
authority, or help someone leave. They can also judge, distract, make demands,
or increase danger. The category *social support* is almost as broad as
*stress*.

A review of experimental emotional-support studies found that its average
stress-reduction estimate did not survive the authors’ main adjustment for
publication bias. A separate experiment with brief support from a stranger
also found no general calming effect. These findings concern particular kinds
of support and measurements, not everything trusted relationships can do.[^2-8]
Someone can matter without lowering your cortisol. They may make the situation
safer, the work lighter, the options wider, or the loneliness less severe. The
useful question is what this person can help change.

The demand may also be built into a workplace, school, home, or public system.
Asking an exhausted person to breathe through an impossible workload mistakes
where the main leverage lies. Yet a program does not work merely because it is
called structural. Workplace efforts to involve employees in changing
conditions have produced uncertain average benefits, and some have added work
to people who already had too much.[^2-9] A committee can become another demand.

Stress cannot tell us whether the person needs practice, rest, treatment,
information, help, protection, a boundary, an exit, or changed conditions. The
word is a prompt to investigate, not an answer.

### Opening the word

When *stress* begins to explain everything, three questions can open it back
up.

**What is being asked of me?** The demand might be performance, uncertainty,
conflict, pain, overload, danger, grief, or the return of something learned in
an earlier situation. Naming it does not make it smaller. It tells us what the
alarm is preparing for.

**Where is the cost showing up?** The answer might be lost sleep, pain, fear,
anger, poor concentration, conflict, avoidance, exhaustion, or an inability to
do what matters. A calmer measurement is not the obvious goal if the schedule
remains impossible or the relationship unsafe.

**What would reach it—and who can do that?** Sometimes a person can act
directly. Sometimes another person has the information, time, money, skill, or
authority. Sometimes no available action can remove the main demand, and the
honest aim is partial: protect sleep, reduce one cost, share vigilance, or make
room for ordinary life.

These are not diagnostic questions, and they do not promise a solution. They
separate the response from the demand, the cost, and the location of possible
change.

Streisand eventually returned to paid performance. Her interviews do not tell
us whether the fear disappeared, whether its meaning changed, or which
conditions made action possible again. A person does not have to erase every
alarm before life can widen.

Understanding an alarm does not excuse everything done in its name. Anger with
a reason can still hurt someone. Avoidance that once protected can shrink a
life. But when we can see what a response is preparing for, we have a better
chance of working at the right level—with less shame about the response, more
responsibility for its consequences, and more honesty about what one person
can change alone.

A life widened beyond vigilance needs something to move toward. Yet pursuit
has its own puzzles, including the possibility of continuing long after the
pleasure has gone.
