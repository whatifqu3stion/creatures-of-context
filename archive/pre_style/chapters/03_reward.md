# Chapter 3
## Wanting more

Imagine that you have stopped enjoying the videos.

You are still watching. Nothing has forced the decision. There is simply another one, already beginning, and the next might be better. If asked whether this is what you want to do with the evening, you might say no while making the small movement that continues it.

Perhaps the watching is soothing, habitual, informative, or preferable to an unpleasant alternative. Continued use alone cannot identify addiction. It does expose a puzzle: we can keep pursuing something while getting little pleasure from it.

To understand the problem, we need to separate attraction, pleasure, and learning.

### The approach and the experience

A smell from a kitchen can make food compelling before the first bite. Eating then brings an experience that may or may not live up to the invitation. Later, the smell may acquire new significance because of what happened.

Researchers use more precise terms for parts of this sequence. *Liking* describes hedonic impact, the pleasurable component of a reward. *Wanting*, in incentive-salience research, describes a process that makes a reward or its cues motivationally attractive. Learning concerns the relationships acquired through experience. These processes interact but can be separated experimentally.[^3a]

The distinction is not a claim that pleasure and desire normally have nothing to do with each other. Their frequent agreement helps make the disagreements interesting. You can be pulled toward an outcome that disappoints you, or enjoy an experience you did not strongly anticipate.

Berridge and Robinson's incentive-sensitization theory addresses a particularly serious version of this separation in addiction. It proposes that, in susceptible individuals, repeated drug exposure can sensitize systems involved in cue-triggered wanting without a matching increase in pleasure. This is a specific account supported by animal and human research, with continuing questions about its scope. It does not mean that every repeated behavior is an addiction or that looking at a phone proves drug-like sensitization.[^3b]

The ordinary implication is less dramatic and more useful: ask separately what attracts you, what you experience while doing it, and how you feel about having done it afterward. These questions need not produce the same answer.

A difficult rehearsal may feel frustrating and leave you glad you attended. A purchase may be thrilling until it arrives. A conversation may begin reluctantly and become the best part of the day. Pleasure is one important outcome; it is not the only reason an activity can deserve a place in a life.

### What surprise can teach

Dopamine often appears in popular explanations as a quantity of pleasure delivered to the brain. That label is too crude to explain the research.

Wolfram Schultz's work on reward prediction errors describes changes in the activity of many dopamine neurons when outcomes differ from expectations. In classic learning tasks, an unexpectedly good outcome produces one pattern, a fully predicted outcome another, and the omission of an expected reward another. As learning develops, predictive cues become important. The discrepancy helps an organism update what to expect.[^3c]

A prediction error is a difference between an outcome and a prediction, not proof of a mistake in judgment or a defect in character. The same broad logic makes learning possible. If every event taught us equally much, whether expected or surprising, experience would be a poor guide.

This explanation also needs its boundaries. Dopamine has multiple roles, and dopamine neurons are not a uniform population. A learning model does not provide a direct reading of what a particular person experiences. Nor does it let us calculate how much dopamine a notification has released by looking at the screen.

The attraction of the chemical story is understandable. It seems to locate a difficult behavior in something concrete. Yet an explanation can sound more scientific while becoming less informative. “Dopamine made me do it” leaves unanswered what the person expected, which cue mattered, what alternatives were available, and why stopping was difficult on that occasion.

Consider an imagined online auction. A person may keep checking because an outcome is genuinely uncertain and financially important. Another may enjoy the competition. A third may be trying to avoid the feeling of losing something already mentally possessed. Reducing all three to a chemical hit would erase the differences that matter for deciding what to change.

### An environment with no last page

A printed book has physical stopping points. A streaming service can begin the next episode while you are deciding whether to continue. A shop has opening hours; an online shop can accompany you into bed.

These are differences in the arrangement of opportunities. Their effects do not require a claim that technology has invented a new human appetite. Removing a pause, placing an invitation within reach, or making an unfinished possibility visible changes the decision a person encounters.

Imagine two versions of a video app. One asks you to choose a video, plays it, and ends. The other begins another video automatically and offers an unlimited sequence of alternatives. Both may contain material you value. They differ in how often stopping is made into an active task.

When a service carries the next action forward, stopping becomes the user’s task. An explicit pause can make that decision visible again. This is the design argument proposed here; the effect of any particular change still has to be examined.

The consequences also depend on what the technology supplies. For someone who is isolated, an online community may be a valued source of companionship. For someone learning a skill, abundant demonstrations can be a gift. A tool can extend a capacity and make overuse easier within the same design.

Hunt Allcott and colleagues tested one bounded change by randomly assigning participants to deactivate Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 United States midterm election. Deactivation improved average subjective wellbeing and changed how participants used their time. It also reduced factual news knowledge. People did not simply exchange the platform for an unbroken sequence of fulfilling social encounters; time shifted into activities including television and time with family and friends.[^3d]

That mixture matters. It gives us evidence that access can affect experience, while refusing a universal story about screens. The study concerned a particular platform, period, intervention, and group of participants. It did not establish what would happen if every digital service disappeared or which individual would benefit from leaving.

A better question than whether technology is good or bad is what a particular pattern of use contributes and displaces.

### What is the activity doing for you?

Suppose you want to stop checking a shopping app.

Start with an occasion rather than a judgment about your personality. When did you open it? What were you expecting? What else was happening? Which part felt compelling: looking, choosing, buying, waiting for delivery, or imagining a different version of your life?

If browsing is a pleasant hobby within your budget, the problem may be smaller than a general rule about consumption suggests. If it repeatedly creates debt, secrecy, or distress, the consequences deserve attention regardless of whether the behavior fits a diagnostic category. If it fills the only unclaimed half hour in a demanding day, removing it without considering what the half hour needs to provide may leave an important question unanswered.

This is where Threat can enter an apparent reward problem. An activity may offer relief from worry or from a task that feels exposing. Meaning can enter too: the purchase may promise membership, competence, beauty, or recognition. The product is sometimes only one part of what is being sought.

These are hypotheses to examine, not hidden motives to announce on somebody else's behalf. It is easy to become overconfident when interpreting desire. A person who wants a better coat may simply need a warmer coat.

The practical inquiry becomes more useful when it remains specific. What good is the activity supplying? What cost is appearing? Could the good be obtained with fewer costs, or is the activity itself worth limiting?

### Putting a decision back into the sequence

A modest experiment might remove one invitation. Turn off a nonessential notification. Sign out after using a service. Decide what you intend to watch before opening the app. Put a purchase on a list and return to it later. These are ways of changing cues and adding a moment for choice; they are not treatments for addiction or methods of cleansing a neurotransmitter.

The result to watch is the outcome you care about. Did you sleep at the intended time? Spend within the amount you chose? Enjoy the activity more? Become less interrupted during a conversation? A lower screen-time number is useful only if it reflects a change worth making.

Be equally interested in an experiment that disappoints you. If removing an app leaves you cut off from friends, the next move may be to preserve those connections through a more deliberate route. If adding friction leads to repeated workarounds, ask whether the barrier is poorly chosen or whether the need being met has been ignored.

You may decide that an activity belongs in a bounded place rather than outside your life. An evening of entertainment can be exactly what was wanted. The relevant distinction is whether the pattern remains compatible with the life it is part of.

Where repeated use brings substantial harm and attempts to change it keep failing, support may be needed. An ordinary environmental adjustment should not be oversold as an adequate response to a clinical disorder. Professional care and practical help can be part of agency, especially when the costs have become difficult to manage alone.

### The appetite for a future

It would be a strange conclusion to this chapter if the answer to excessive pursuit were to become a person who wanted less of everything.

Curiosity, affection, ambition, play, and hope also draw us forward. A good environment should make room for pursuits we are glad to have followed. The problem is often not the existence of wanting but the repeated separation between what draws us in and what we endorse when we have a chance to consider it.

That separation gives us somewhere to investigate. The next invitation can be changed. The pause can be restored. The desired good can be named more accurately. At other times, the deeper work concerns what experience has taught a person to expect from the world.

The same promise will not attract everyone. Some people have learned that waiting pays; others have reasons to be skeptical. Some expect approach to bring interest and welcome; others have learned to watch for the cost.

To understand those differences, we have to go back to how a world becomes familiar.
