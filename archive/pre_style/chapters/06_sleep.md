# Chapter 6
## A place for sleep

Trying hard is an odd way to fall asleep.

You can turn off a light, put down a book, and decide that work is finished. You cannot carry out sleep with the same obedience. Lying awake, you calculate how many hours remain before morning. The arithmetic is correct. It is also becoming another thing to do in the dark.

Well before that calculation, however, there was a decision about when the evening would end. Sometimes that is where the more revealing story begins.

Take an invented household. At half past ten, a parent finally sits down with a phone. The children are asleep. The kitchen is usable again. There is school paperwork to finish, but it can wait until morning. For the first time since breakfast, nobody is asking for anything.

The parent has already decided to go to bed earlier. Now the quiet has arrived, and giving it up feels like ending the day without having had a part in it. A funny message from a friend becomes a conversation. There is time for one more thing, then another. The alarm will ring at the same hour regardless.

We could call this a failure to stop. We would still need to understand what stopping would surrender.

### Two kinds of time

Sleep researchers distinguish the pressure that builds with wakefulness from the daily timing of sleep and alertness. In Alexander Borbély and colleagues’ two-process model, the first rises while we are awake and declines during sleep. The second follows a circadian rhythm: an approximately daily cycle that changes our readiness for sleep. Together, they help explain why hours awake alone cannot tell us when someone will fall asleep.[^6a]

A nap illustrates the interaction. Sleep during the day reduces some of the accumulated pressure; it does not simply reset the body’s daily timing to morning. Later, the same clock time can meet a different history of wakefulness. The model gives us a way to understand that difference, though it cannot explain every cause of a difficult night.

The surroundings enter this biology. In a small experiment, Anne-Marie Chang and colleagues had twelve healthy young adults read for about four hours before bed, for five evenings on a light-emitting tablet and five with printed books. Everyone completed both conditions, in a randomly assigned order. The room was dim and the tablet at maximum brightness. After the tablet condition, participants’ melatonin rhythm was later, they took longer to fall asleep, and they felt sleepier the next morning.[^6b]

Those conditions matter: this was prolonged exposure, not a quick check of a message. Nor did the experiment isolate light’s color from its intensity. It gives us a concrete reason to take evening light seriously, without supplying a universal screen curfew.

Back in our imagined household, the phone also contains a friend. Moving it out of the bedroom might help the parent stop checking it. But if the conversation simply moves to a laptop in the kitchen, the bedtime has not moved at all. Changing screen color cannot create an hour that has been spent elsewhere.

Suppose the parent tries leaving the phone to charge downstairs. On the first evening, that removes the easy continuation of the conversation. On another, the parent stays up with a printed book instead. There is pleasure in the book, and reluctance to close it. The first attempt has made one explanation less convincing: the phone’s presence cannot be the whole problem.

### An hour that belongs to somebody

The most obvious solution is to move free time earlier. In this household, that means moving something else.

The other parent is also tired. Taking over the kitchen every evening would give one person an earlier break by postponing the other’s. The school paperwork still needs doing. Tomorrow’s meals will still need to be made. Even a modest change raises a question that a bedtime reminder cannot answer: whose obligation can move, and where will it go?

The parents try a smaller arrangement. On alternate evenings, one takes the remaining kitchen work while the other gets half an hour alone. They agree to leave the clean laundry unfolded. That means living with the basket in the bedroom for another day, which bothers one of them more than the other.

The first week goes unevenly. On one evening the protected time is used for the school paperwork. On another, a child needs attention. On a third, the parent reads for pleasure before ten and closes the book at the end of a chapter. The revised arrangement has created some room. It has also exposed a habit of treating unclaimed time as space for another obligation.

The next decision is more specific than “be disciplined.” Can the paperwork be dealt with during the shared work period? When an interruption consumes someone’s break, is there another opportunity? Does a finished chapter provide an ending the parent is willing to keep?

There is still a choice to make when the book is open. An explanation of the late night does not close it. The parent may decide that tonight’s pleasure is worth tomorrow’s cost, or find that a plan needs practice before it becomes easy to follow. The useful change is that sleep no longer requires giving up every moment of chosen time.

This household has another adult available and some discretion over the evening. A parent caring alone may have neither. Someone whose shift changes without notice cannot privately manufacture a predictable week. In those circumstances, advice to negotiate a better bedtime can become another demand placed on the person with the least power to meet it. The practical question reaches whoever controls the schedule, the care, or the resources.

And the cost of short sleep needs more than a guess based on how familiar it feels.

In a laboratory study, Hans Van Dongen and colleagues randomly assigned healthy adults to four, six, or eight hours in bed each night for fourteen nights. During waking hours, they repeatedly tested performance and asked participants how sleepy they felt. One test required quick responses to signals appearing at unpredictable intervals. In the four- and six-hour groups, lapses accumulated across the days, while reported sleepiness rose initially and then changed much less.[^6c]

The two records told different stories. Feeling accustomed to a schedule was poor assurance that performance had stopped deteriorating. The experiment does not set an exact sleep requirement for every reader; it does challenge the comforting inference that a familiar cost has disappeared.

For our parent, this means that “I can get through tomorrow” cannot settle the matter. It also cannot decide how a household should share its work. A laboratory can help reveal a cost. People still have to decide who will carry it.

### When there is room, but sleep will not come

There is another possible continuation of the parent’s story. The work is shared, some chosen time happens earlier, and the parent goes to bed. Then comes the arithmetic in the dark.

An opportunity to sleep and the ability to sleep are different. If we insist that every difficult night must be explained by the household arrangement, we can become as unhelpful as the person who insists that every problem is a lack of willpower. An explanation earns its place by helping us understand what is happening. It should be possible to discover that we need a different one.

For adults with chronic insomnia disorder, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends multicomponent cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I. It conditionally recommends against sleep hygiene as the only treatment.[^6d] General advice about routines and surroundings can therefore leave the central problem untreated. CBT-I is a structured treatment; making a bedroom darker is not its equivalent.

Persistent sleep difficulty with daytime impairment deserves clinical assessment. The assessment can also consider other sleep disorders and health conditions.[^6e] At that point, the parent needs more than increasingly elaborate instructions for conducting the evening correctly.

Calling all of these problems “sleep habits” makes them easier to discuss together and harder to distinguish when somebody needs help. A changed schedule cannot do the work of a treatment, and a treatment cannot give a shift worker authority over the rota.

We should also ask what would count as a worthwhile result for the household. An earlier bedtime alone is an incomplete answer if it is purchased by quietly taking time from the other parent. Nor should every evening become an inspection of whether everyone has prepared for sleep perfectly. There is room for an absorbing book, a conversation that runs late, and the ordinary untidiness of living together.

The aim is a recurring arrangement in which rest has a place and people can notice when that arrangement is failing. Some of the work belongs to the person who must eventually put the book down. Some belongs to the people whose requests keep arriving. Some may require help that the household cannot provide for itself.

I would not make rest earn its place solely by improving tomorrow’s output. A person deserves care even when no increase in productivity follows. That is an ethical judgment, and the laboratory cannot make it for us.

At half past ten, the clean laundry can remain in its basket. It will need attention tomorrow. The people who left it there need attention tonight.
