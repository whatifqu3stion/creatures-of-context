# Chapter 5
## Averages do not live your life

An average is a useful answer to a particular question. It becomes troublesome when asked to describe a person.

Suppose a study finds that a group benefits from a certain change in routine. That finding can give you a good reason to consider the change. It cannot tell you, by itself, how much you will benefit, what it will cost you, or whether an important feature of your life was represented in the study.

We know this in ordinary settings. Shoes can be the right size on average and still fail to fit a foot. In psychological explanations, however, an average result can acquire the authority of a rule. When the rule fails, people are left wondering whether they have performed their humanity incorrectly.

The problem is not that research uses averages. Much important knowledge depends on them. The problem is forgetting which question the average answered.

### A person contains variation too

Personality descriptions often sound like fixed instructions. Someone is outgoing, cautious, conscientious, or emotionally reactive, as though each label specified what happens whenever a situation appears.

William Fleeson studied personality-related states repeatedly during ordinary life. Across his experience-sampling studies, people showed considerable variation in how they behaved, while differing more stably in the average patterns around which their behavior ranged.[^5a] A person could be relatively outgoing compared with others and still have many quiet moments.

A trait summarizes a pattern rather than specifying every moment. Imagine someone who speaks freely among close friends and says little in a formal meeting. The two observations are not necessarily inconsistent. The relationships, expectations, knowledge, and consequences differ. A label such as *introverted* may describe a broad tendency while doing little to explain the contrast between those settings.

The same caution applies to self-description. “I am bad at starting things” might conceal a useful pattern: perhaps starting is difficult when instructions are ambiguous, when the work will be judged publicly, or when there is no clear first action. In other circumstances, the same person may begin readily.

Replacing a total description with a conditional one can expose something that can be investigated. It need not erase the difficulty or claim that everyone has the same capacities.

### What genes do and do not tell us

Genetic differences contribute to variation in many human traits. Tinca Polderman and colleagues' large synthesis of twin research documents the breadth of that contribution across the traits studied.[^5b] An account of context that excluded inheritance would be incomplete.

But *heritable* is easily misread as *unchangeable*.

Heritability describes variation within a studied population under particular conditions. It is not the percentage of an individual's trait caused by genes. Its value can differ when the population or environments differ, and a high estimate does not tell us that an environmental intervention would be ineffective.[^5c]

Consider the logic without attaching a number to a person. If members of a population have similar access to an important resource, differences in that resource will explain little of the variation observed there. That does not establish that the resource is unnecessary. Changing its availability could alter outcomes substantially.

Twin research also relies on assumptions about genetic resemblance, environmental resemblance, and measurement. It can inform an account of differences without authorizing a story about a specific person's biological destiny.

For ordinary decisions, this distinction is liberating in a restrained way. You do not have to deny a tendency in order to seek conditions that help. Nor do you have to prove that a difficulty is genetically caused before it deserves accommodation.

The useful question is what reliably improves participation or reduces a meaningful cost.

### Sensitivity is not a rank

People also differ in how strongly they respond to aspects of their surroundings.

Michael Pluess has brought together research frameworks concerning environmental sensitivity, including the possibility that some individuals are more affected by supportive as well as adverse conditions.[^5d] It is an important alternative to thinking of sensitivity only as vulnerability. It is also a field with difficult questions about measurement, mechanisms, and the reliability of particular interactions.

The idea should not become a sorting ceremony in which everyone is assigned a flower, a nervous-system type, or a predicted future. A vivid category can outrun the evidence and obscure differences within the category.

A person may find one kind of stimulation difficult and another welcome. Noise, unpredictability, criticism, social density, physical exertion, and novelty are not a single substance delivered at different strengths. Describing which conditions matter is more informative than saying that someone is sensitive to life.

The setting has assumptions of its own. If a workplace provides only one way to concentrate, communicate, or recover, a difficulty attributed to an individual may partly reflect the narrowness of the available options.

That observation does not mean every demand can be removed. Jobs, relationships, and shared spaces require coordination. The aim is to distinguish an unavoidable requirement from a convention that has never been questioned.

### Change without self-erasure

Traits can be persistent without being frozen.

Brent Roberts and colleagues reviewed studies of personality change during interventions, many of them clinical. Measured changes occurred, particularly in emotional stability, although designs and interventions varied.[^5e] The evidence challenges the idea that adult personality cannot change. It does not establish that any person can choose any trait or that change is effortless.

There are at least two different aims here. One is to develop a capacity: become more able to speak, recover, plan, or tolerate an unfamiliar situation. Another is to arrange life so that an existing difficulty creates less unnecessary cost. The aims can coexist.

Imagine a person who finds an unstructured networking event overwhelming but can have a thoughtful conversation when there is a shared task. They might practice entering less familiar conversations. They might also choose settings where an activity gives the encounter a shape. Calling the second option avoidance would be premature. It may be a sensible way to participate.

On the other hand, a pattern of progressively narrowing life deserves attention. An arrangement that protects a valued activity is different from a rule that steadily makes valued activities impossible. The distinction depends on what the person wants to remain able to do, not on whether the arrangement looks conventional.

Fit should expand a life where possible. It should not become an elegant explanation for why a person must never encounter difficulty.

### A modest science of your own circumstances

You do not need to settle your personality before trying a change.

Choose a recurring difficulty and describe it closely. Note when it happens, what precedes it, what helps, and what outcome matters. If possible, vary one condition for long enough to encounter it more than once. A quieter place, clearer instructions, a different time, or a conversation in advance may reveal something useful.

This is practical observation, not a controlled trial. Expectations, chance, changing demands, and several simultaneous changes can confuse the result. The aim is not to claim scientific certainty from a week of notes. It is to make a better next decision than a global judgment about character would allow.

An experiment can also reveal a limit. If a difficulty persists across settings, or if it interferes substantially with life, a professional assessment may clarify issues that a general framework cannot. Accommodations, assistive tools, treatment, and help from other people need not wait for a complete theory of the person.

There is an ethical boundary too. Individual variation should never become a reason to tolerate preventable harm. The fact that some people cope well with a demand does not establish that the demand is acceptable. A dangerous workplace is not redeemed by the resilience of the workers who survive it.

We can hold both ideas: people differ, and conditions can be improved for everyone.

The same care is needed with everyday advice. A real biological need can be shared while the opportunities to meet it differ. At the end of a long day, knowing that sleep matters may be the easiest part of getting it.
