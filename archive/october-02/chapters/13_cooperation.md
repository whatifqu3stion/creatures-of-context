# Chapter 13
## The stories we tell about one another

Suppose several neighbors share a small garden. They contribute different amounts of time, have different abilities, and do not always agree about what the space is for.

One person seems never to water. Another takes more produce than expected. Somebody explains that the first person has been caring for an ill partner; somebody else disputes the explanation. Soon the group is discussing not only vegetables but character.

A meeting produces an awkward discovery. There was a watering list, but nobody kept it current. The supposed limit on produce was something several members thought everybody understood. Two people have been doing much of the work, and they are tired of correcting the gaps. Others did not know how large the gaps had become.

The group has real grievances and uncertain information. It needs a decision before the next dry week.

### What a reputation tells us

We cannot witness everything another person does. Information from others helps us decide whom to trust and what arrangements need attention.

Matthew Feinberg, Robb Willer, and Michael Schultz tested gossip and exclusion in a laboratory public-goods setting. Participants could contribute to a shared fund, and in some conditions information about behavior could reach future partners. When participants could use reputational information to exclude others, the arrangements supported greater cooperation in the task.[^13a]

The study made certain contributions visible. In the garden, care elsewhere may be invisible, and a persuasive storyteller may be wrong. The result gives us a reason to take reputational information seriously; it does not tell us which neighbor to believe.

Dismissing every conversation about an absent person as malicious would remove a way to share warnings and recognize reliability. Believing every accusation would create another problem. We need to know what information is being passed and what others are invited to do with it.

There is a considerable distance between reporting that someone missed a task and declaring them selfish. The first claim can be checked. The second proposes a general account of the person. Once accepted, that account can absorb almost anything: another absence confirms it, while a helpful act is dismissed as a performance.

In this garden, the neighbor caring for an ill partner can still answer a specific question about an agreed task. Care responsibilities do not tell us whether a particular promise was made or kept. They do matter when deciding what promise can reasonably be made next.

A group needs room for both kinds of information. Immediate protection may be necessary when harm is credibly reported; fairness does not require leaving people exposed until uncertainty disappears. It does require keeping observation distinct from inference and leaving a route for an account to be corrected.

### A clear rule, at last

The gardeners agree that each member will do two hours a week. They put the rota where everybody can see it. Two missed weeks will lead to a review of membership. Produce will be shared at a regular collection time, with a stated limit while supplies are short.

For the people who have been carrying the work, the decision is a relief. Expectations can now be pointed to. A missed task no longer requires a fresh argument about whether it was anybody’s responsibility.

For the caregiver, a fixed block away from home is difficult. Another member can manage watering but cannot do two hours of digging. The equal-hours rule has made one kind of fairness visible while neglecting differences in what the hours require.

That does not make the rule absurd. Nor does it mean the two overworked members should quietly resume filling every gap. The group has reached a conflict between legitimate concerns: dependable work, usable access, and a limit on what anyone can be expected to absorb.

Punishment cannot settle that conflict by itself. Benedikt Herrmann, Christian Thöni, and Simon Gächter compared public-goods experiments across sixteen participant pools. Some participants punished high contributors, and in some pools this antisocial punishment undermined the cooperation-enhancing effect of punishment.[^13e] A sanction is not automatically aimed at the behavior an observer wants to discourage. These findings do not reveal the motives of our imagined neighbors; they make it harder to treat the power to punish as a complete solution.

The garden’s new rule therefore needs more than an enforcement mechanism. Members need a way to say what it requires of them before missing a shift becomes proof that they do not care.

### Repair has to mean something

The member who took extra produce acknowledges doing so. But because the old limit was never clearly agreed, the group cannot honestly describe this as the violation of a rule everyone knew. It can explain the shortage, make the new limit explicit, and ask that it be followed.

Suppose the same person takes extra again after agreeing to the limit. The information has changed. There is now a particular promise to discuss and a loss borne by other members. Repair might involve replacing what was taken or accepting restricted access. It need not begin with a verdict about the person’s entire character.

June Tangney, Jeff Stuewig, and Debra Mashek review a distinction between guilt focused on a particular action and shame focused more globally on the self. In the literature they discuss, guilt is often linked with reparative tendencies, while shame can be associated with concealment or defensiveness.[^13b] These patterns do not establish whether a feeling is deserved. They do help us ask whether distress is bringing the consequences into view or burying them beneath a judgment of the self.

Declaring oneself a terrible person can sound severe while leaving everyone else’s losses untouched. Conversely, a group can claim to want repair while making repair impossible. If every future contribution will be read as manipulation, what would count as becoming trustworthy again?

Some harms justify lasting limits, and a harmed person does not owe reconciliation. Where changed conduct is the aim, however, the response should make clear what change matters. Otherwise the punishment can continue long after anyone has stopped asking what it is supposed to achieve.

### When the argument acquires an audience

A message about the repeated shortage reaches the neighborhood group. Replies arrive from people who have never seen the rota. The accusation is easier to follow than the history of how the rule was made.

William Brady and colleagues combined observational studies of Twitter with behavioral experiments to examine moral outrage expression. Their findings linked social feedback and perceived network norms to subsequent expressions of outrage.[^13c] The outcome was expression; the researchers were not reading each person’s private emotional life from a post.

A forceful statement may reflect sincere anger, adaptation to an audience, or both. In the garden dispute, applause might encourage a member to repeat the simplest accusation, even after the meeting has revealed a more complicated account. The person need not be pretending to be angry for the audience to influence what they say next.

The audience also has choices. It can ask what was agreed, make space for an answer, or reward the satisfaction of seeing a disliked person reduced to a type. Attention can make a style of participation worth repeating. It cannot establish that the story is true.

### What fairness asks of the group

Laboratory games are sometimes recruited to announce what humans are really like: selfish, generous, punitive, fair. Joseph Henrich and colleagues’ work across fifteen small-scale societies instead found substantial variation in economic-game behavior, with patterns related to features of everyday social and economic life.[^13d] One familiar setting cannot silently supply the human baseline.

Our gardeners also bring different expectations. Equal hours, equal benefit, contribution according to capacity, and priority according to need are distinct principles. Agreement that everybody should be fair does not tell them which principle governs the watering or the harvest.

At the next meeting, the group separates those decisions. First it lists the jobs that must actually be done. Some need strength, some need brief visits, and some can be done from home. The caregiver offers to maintain the rota and arrange swaps. The members who have been doing most of the watering want to know who will cover a gap if that arrangement fails.

Their question deserves an answer. A proposal to recognize hidden work can still leave visible work undone. The group agrees to try assigning tasks and review the arrangement after a month, recording unfilled jobs as well as completed ones. That creates a way to learn whether the new division is workable. It also creates a new job: keeping the record.

No experiment cited here tells the gardeners exactly how to divide those obligations. They must use what they know, hear from the people affected, and remain willing to revise a decision. Cooperation depends partly on how they manage the next failure, after the relief of making a rule has passed.

Eventually a newcomer will ask how the garden works. Somebody will explain the rota, the limits, and perhaps the argument that produced them. By then the group will have made more than a decision. It will have made something to hand on.
