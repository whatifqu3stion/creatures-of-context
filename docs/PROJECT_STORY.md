# Why these chapters belong together

The book asks what changes when we look at a person's behavior alongside their circumstances. That includes their body, experience, relationships, and the choices available to them.

It is a broad subject. Stress, sleep, habits, and culture could easily become separate explainers with little reason to read them in order. The three-part structure gives them a shared direction.

| Part | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Systems in context | What might explain a response, and what evidence would help us distinguish the possibilities? |
| Everyday environments | What happens when an intention meets the actual arrangements of a day? |
| Shared worlds | Who sets those arrangements, who carries their costs, and what can people change together? |

The final section matters because a book about circumstances could otherwise end by giving everyone more instructions for managing themselves. Cooperation and culture bring other people's decisions into view: an individual cannot personally change every condition affecting them.

Threat, reward, and meaning are starting questions, not a model every chapter must reproduce. Later chapters test the limits of explanation: knowing what an arrangement costs does not settle who should pay, and shared understanding can leave a conflict intact. The three questions are not a validated scientific model or three separate brain systems.

Whether this structure carries a reader through the book still needs outside feedback. The [process account](../methods/WORKFLOW.md) describes how we drafted and revised it; the [worked examples](../methods/WORKED_EXAMPLES.md) show particular changes.
